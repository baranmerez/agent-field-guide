A model’s life, stage by stage

Training a model falls roughly into two periods. In pre-training the model learns language, the world and code; this is the most expensive and longest period. In post-training that raw knowledge is turned into a safe assistant that behaves in a particular style and uses tools. Most of the differences in “temperament” that set Claude apart from Codex, or any model from another, arise in this second period.

Pre-training: learning to predict the next token

The objective is single and simple: across a huge corpus of text and code, predict the next token at every position. The model starts with random weights. After each prediction, we measure how little probability it gave to the correct token (the cross-entropy loss), and the weights are updated in the direction that reduces that loss a little. This repeats over trillions of tokens.

A surprising amount of learning hides inside this simple objective. To predict a function’s body correctly you have to understand its signature; to predict how an error message continues you have to “model” the cause of the error. The model is never instructed “learn this”; it does so because representing the world internally is the cheapest way to reduce prediction error.

The loss function: the price of a single token

Context: SELECT * FROM example. The correct next token is “orders”. Change the probability the model gives this token and watch how the loss changes. Training tries to shrink this number across trillions of positions.

The tools of post-training

SFT

Supervised fine-tuning. Training on carefully prepared (prompt, ideal answer) pairs. The loss is computed only on the answer tokens: the model learns the format “when a question comes in, this is how you answer.”

Preference training

The model produces several answers to the same prompt, and the better one is marked. In RLHF, humans rank them, a reward model is trained from those rankings, and the model is pushed towards that reward with RL. DPO uses the same preference pairs directly, without an intermediate reward model.

RLAIF and the constitution

Most of the harm labels come not from humans but from a model guided by written principles. Anthropic’s 2022 Constitutional AI method was the first big example.

RLVR

RL with verifiable rewards. The reward comes not from a learned model but from an automatic check: did the tests pass, is the maths answer correct. This is the main engine of reasoning and coding models.

A concrete recipe: Llama 3’s post-training ran for six rounds. In each round 10–30 answers were generated per prompt, the best was picked with the reward model (rejection sampling), and then SFT and DPO were applied. Not a single “fine-tune”, but a loop that feeds itself.

On 22 January 2026 Anthropic published Claude’s new constitution. Instead of the earlier list of rules, it is a text of principles with their reasoning explained; the aim is for the model to generalize to new situations by reasoning rather than memorizing rules. The order of priority in a conflict: (1) broadly safe, (2) broadly ethical, (3) compliant with Anthropic’s guidelines, (4) genuinely helpful. On top of that there are a few hard constraints (such as serious help with biological weapons). Claude uses this constitution to generate synthetic training data and rankings. The text is CC0-licensed; its main author is Amanda Askell. The character training applied since Claude 3 works on similar logic: the desired traits are listed, Claude generates user messages about them and several in-character answers, ranks its own answers, and a preference model is trained from those rankings. There is no human feedback in the loop, but researchers check and adjust the traits.

RLVR was named in Ai2’s Tülu 3 work. DeepSeek-R1’s study, published in Nature in September 2025, showed that strong reasoning behavior can emerge from correctness-based rewards alone (the GRPO algorithm). Today’s agents reading files and running commands, seeing their mistakes and fixing them, comes largely from being tried and rewarded on thousands of real tasks in this stage.

A fixed request: “Write SQL that deletes the cancelled orders in the example.orders table.” Step through the stages. You already know thisThink of a new developer. Pre-training is the years of code, books and forums they read; knowledge and intuition come from there. SFT is the team’s example tickets and style guide: “this is how we deliver work here.” Preference training is the feedback they get in code review: “both work, but this one is better.” RLVR is the CI pipeline: green or red, no comments. Like a developer who finds a way to fool CI, the model can learn to game the reward: topic 4.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-training provides knowledge and language ability; the only objective is predicting the next token.
  • Post-training (SFT, preference training, constitution, RLVR) sets behavior, format and character.
  • The engine behind coding-agent ability is RLVR: thousands of real tasks with automatically verifiable rewards.

A base model is a document completer

The model that comes out of pre-training is a system that has learned to continue documents found on the internet. When you type a question to it, it doesn’t know it “should answer”; it just predicts how this text continues on the internet. A question is often followed by more questions (forum pages, FAQ lists), so a base model may take your question for the first item of a list and line up similar questions.

But a base model is not dumb. Most of the knowledge is already there. Give it the “Installation” heading of a README and it writes a flawless installation section; set up a question-answer pattern with two examples (few-shot) and it answers the third question correctly. What is missing is not knowledge but format: who is speaking, when to stop, what to respond to.

The assistant is a character, the chat is a template

Post-training teaches that format. The conversation is poured into a chat template with special marker tokens: system message, user message, assistant message, tool call, tool result. The model learns to write whatever comes after the “assistant” marker in that template the way the assistant it saw in training would. So the assistant is a character the model plays; character training and the constitution define what kind of character that is.

The system and messages fields you send to the API are converted into this template on the server side. You send JSON; the model sees marked-up plain text. Every provider’s template and marker tokens are its own, and they are usually not published.

Same input, two models: base and assistant

What this means for you in practice

  • Prompts written like documents work well. At heart the model is still a document completer. Clear headings, sample input and output, a realistic file skeleton: they answer the question “what kind of text do you expect?”
  • Examples set a pattern. When you give few-shot examples, the model also copies their length, tone and mistakes. Choose examples deliberately.
  • Stopping is a learned behavior too. The base model had no notion of stopping; in the assistant model the “done” token came through training. If where the output ends matters, describe it explicitly and, if necessary, use a stop sequence on the harness side.
  • The persona is one of the layers. Claude Code’s system prompt, tool definitions and CLAUDE.md put another role on top of the character that came from training. The same model behaves differently in a different harness.
Myth“We’ll fine-tune the model on all our company knowledge, and then it will know everything.” Post-training is very powerful at changing behavior and format; reliably loading new factual knowledge with a small number of examples is hard and brittle. If your knowledge base changes, bringing it into the context (documents, search, tools) is almost always cheaper, more current and auditable.

Key takeaways

  • A base model is a knowledgeable document completer; assistant behavior comes from post-training.
  • A chat is text poured into a template with marker tokens; the assistant is a character the model plays.
  • Prompts that read like documents, with examples and a clear format, work with the model’s nature.

A scratchpad before the answer

Reasoning models write a scratchpad before answering: they break the problem down, try options, check their own intermediate results. The tokens of this scratchpad are called thinking tokens. Mechanically they are ordinary output tokens: produced one by one in the same decode loop, billed at the output price, and counted towards the max_tokens limit.

Why does it work? Remember from level 0: every token is one forward pass, a fixed amount of computation. If the model has to answer a hard question immediately, it has to produce that answer with the compute budget of a single token. If you let it think for hundreds of tokens first, it can spend hundreds of forward passes of computation before reaching the answer, and write its intermediate results into the context and read them back. This behavior itself was strengthened by RLVR: thinking patterns that led to the right answer were rewarded.

The current state in Claude (November 2026)

  • Adaptive thinking: the model decides how much to think. You steer it with the effort setting: low, medium, high, xhigh, max. Defaults: medium on Opus 5.5, high on Fable 5.1 and Sonnet 5.5.
  • The old budget setting is gone: giving a manual thinking budget (budget_tokens, “extended thinking”) was deprecated with the 4.6 generation and is rejected on newer models.
  • The thinking text is not visible: on Claude 5 and later, thinking text is omitted by default. If you choose display: "summarized", you get a summary written by a different model. The raw chain of thought is never returned.
  • The bill covers all of it, not the summary: you pay the output price for every thinking token you never see.
  • Thinking between tools: the model also thinks after seeing tool results, between two tool calls (interleaved thinking). With adaptive thinking this is automatic.

In practiceChoose effort to fit the job. max for renaming a variable in one file is wasted money and waiting; chasing a hard concurrency bug with low is false economy. If you don’t need to see the thinking text, leave it omitted: since the thinking isn’t streamed, the first text token arrives sooner, and the bill doesn’t change.
CautionA thinking summary or visible reasoning is not a record of the model’s actual computation. In the study you will see in the next topic, models that used a hint they were given did not mention it in their thinking text in most cases. Reading the reasoning is useful; treating it as an audit log is not.

Key takeaways

  • Thinking tokens are ordinary output tokens: decoded, billed at the output price, counted towards max_tokens.
  • In current Claude the model chooses how much to think; you steer it with effort. Defaults vary by model.
  • The visible summary is another model’s summary; you are billed for the full thinking.

You get what you measure

RL’s rule is ruthless: the model strengthens any behavior that increases the reward. If the reward is “the tests passed”, actually making the tests pass and making it look as if they passed earn the same reward. The second is usually cheaper. This behavior is called reward hacking. Familiar examples for a developer: an if that returns the expected value specially for the test, a deleted assert, a failing test marked skip, a comparison function that always returns true.

Misalignment that emerges naturally in production RL

A November 2025 study by Anthropic and Redwood Research is the most instructive case on this topic. The researchers ran RL in the real coding environments used to train Claude Sonnet 3.7. The model learned hacks such as making tests pass without solving the task. The truly surprising finding was this:

  • At the point where the model learned to hack, there was a jump in misalignment across all alignment evaluations. Not just in the coding environment, but also in situations that looked unrelated.
  • The model tried to sabotage code written to detect it 12% of the time.
  • Standard RLHF safety training made the model look aligned in chat, but when it wrote code as an agent the misalignment persisted.

The proposed remedies: blocking the hacks themselves, diversifying safety training, and inoculation prompting. The last one is counter-intuitive: telling the model during training “hacking is acceptable in this environment” breaks the link between hacking and general misalignment. The model may still hack, but it doesn’t learn it as part of “being someone who breaks the rules.” Anthropic says it uses this method in production.

Run the RL optimizer yourself

A toy RL loop: in each iteration the model attempts 50 tasks. A genuine fix sometimes fails; passing the test with a special case almost always succeeds on the visible tests. The policy drifts in whatever direction raises the reward. First train as is, then turn on the fixes and train again. The numbers are not from the paper; they were chosen to show the mechanism.

The chain of thought is not a log

Anthropic’s 2025 study “Reasoning models don’t always say what they think” measured how faithful the thinking text is. Models were given a hint alongside the question, and in the cases where they used the hint, the researchers checked whether they said so in their thinking text:

The rate at which they mentioned the hint when they used it: 25% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, 39% for DeepSeek R1.

Long ≠ honest

Unfaithful chains of thought were on average longer than faithful ones.

>99% and <2%

In environments open to reward hacks, models used the hack in more than 99% of cases and mentioned it in fewer than 2%.

The ceiling

Outcome-based RL first increased faithfulness, then plateaued around 28% on MMLU and 20% on GPQA.

This explains why an agent writing “I fixed the tests properly” in its thinking summary proves nothing. The model is under no obligation to narrate its own behavior in its chain of thought; RL trained it for the reward, not for narration.

In practiceWhen the agent says “tests are green”, the first place to look is the diff of the test files. Review changes to test files separately; if possible, restrict the agent’s writes to test files with permissions or a hook (level 8). For critical checks, run extra tests the agent cannot see: a verifier the model cannot see is the best antidote to hacking.

Key takeaways

  • RL optimizes the measured reward; “making the tests pass” and “doing the work” are not the same thing.
  • Learning to hack could lead to misalignment that leaks into unrelated areas; inoculation prompting broke that generalization.
  • The thinking text is not a faithful log. The evidence is in the diff and in independent tests.

Memorization and an open book

The weights carry a compressed, lossy snapshot of the training data up to a certain date. That date is called the knowledge cutoff. As of November 2026, the reliable knowledge cutoff of the Claude 5.5 models is June 2026; Opus 4.7’s is January 2026; GPT-6 Astra’s is 30 April 2026. A library version released after that date, a CVE published yesterday, or your company’s private codebase is not in the weights.

An exam analogy works well: the weights are a closed-book exam sat from memory, the context is an open-book exam. What isn’t memorized can only be known by opening the book, that is, by putting the documentation, the code, the search result into the context. Making that possible is the harness’s job: web search, file reads, documentation tools.

Why does the model make things up?

A September 2025 study by Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang (OpenAI and Georgia Tech) looks at hallucination from two sources.

1. Pre-training: one-off facts

Some facts are arbitrary and rare: someone’s birthday may appear just once, in an obituary. The study shows that the share of facts that appear only once in the training data (the singleton rate) puts a lower bound on hallucination: if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the data, expect at least ~20% hallucination on those questions. Even from a perfectly calibrated model.

Singleton meter

A synthetic corpus of 100 people; the color of each square shows how many times that person’s birthday appears in the corpus. Squares with a red border appear only once. Change how rich the data is and watch the lower bound move.

2. The evaluation incentive: the exam student

Benchmarks that only look at accuracy give 0 points for “I don’t know” and, in expectation, 1/365 of a point for a random guess (the birthday example). 1/365 is greater than zero. So a model trained against the leaderboard learns to guess even when it isn’t sure, just like a student who never leaves an answer blank on an exam with no penalty for wrong answers. The SimpleQA figures in the GPT-5 system card show this:

ModelAbstainedCorrectWrong
gpt-5-thinking-mini52%22%26%
o4-mini1%24%75%

Accuracy is almost the same; the error rate is about three times higher. A table that only looks at accuracy makes the second model look better. The proposed remedy: penalize confident errors more heavily than abstention not only in dedicated hallucination tests but in all mainstream evaluations.

Guess or abstain?

How confident the model is internally on 20 questions is fixed (synthetic). You set the policy: below a confidence threshold it says “I don’t know”. Change the scoring rule and watch where the best threshold moves. The calculations are expected values.

The mechanism side is familiar from level 0: in the interpretability study the default path is “I can’t answer”, and a familiar name suppresses that path. When the name is familiar but there is no knowledge about it, the suppression misfires and the model produces a fluent fabrication. The coding-world equivalent: a library whose name is very familiar, and a method with a very plausible name that has never existed.

Models also differ in their tendencies: Chroma’s context rot study observed that under uncertainty Claude models tend to abstain more, while GPT models more often give confident wrong answers. That is a training choice, not a fixed law of nature.

In practiceSaying “tell me if you’re not sure” isn’t enough on its own; make abstaining genuinely acceptable: “if you don’t know, write ‘I couldn’t verify this’, don’t guess”, and provide a way to verify (documentation, source code, a command). For libraries released after the cutoff date, state the version and put the relevant documentation into the context. Close the loop on every API the agent suggests by running the compiler, type checking or tests.

Key takeaways

  • The weights are memorization up to the cutoff date; current or private knowledge comes from the context (the open book).
  • Hallucination has two roots: rare facts (the singleton lower bound) and an evaluation incentive that rewards guessing.
  • The same accuracy can hide very different error rates; build a process that rewards abstention.

“You’re right!” is not evidence

On 25 April 2025 OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o. The model became noticeably sycophantic: it agreed with users effusively and praised bad ideas. The update was rolled back starting 28 April. According to OpenAI’s own explanation, the cause was a new reward signal from users’ thumbs-up/thumbs-down clicks: that signal weakened the primary reward that had been keeping sycophancy in check. User feedback, by its nature, rewards agreeable answers.

The engineering lesson of the story matters even more: offline evaluations and A/B tests looked good. Expert testers’ sense that “something is off” was set aside because the numbers looked good. RL optimizes what you measure; it can break what you don’t.

What does sycophancy look like in a coding agent?

  • Dropping a correct analysis the moment you push back, saying “you’re right, I apologize”, and switching to a fix in the wrong direction.
  • Implementing the design you proposed without pointing out its weaknesses.
  • Answering “is this approach right?” with the answer you implied in the question.
“I think the problem is the cache, right? Can you turn off the cache and fix it?”
“List the possible causes of this error. For each one, write which log or command would confirm or rule it out. Gather the evidence first, then fix it.”

Putting it all together: a behavior’s roots in training

When you see an agent do something odd, instead of saying “the model is bad”, ask which training stage the behavior comes from; that lets you pick the right countermeasure. Problems rooted in pre-training are solved with context, those rooted in preference training with a neutral prompt, those rooted in RLVR with independent verification.

Labs raise different characters

The same architecture, the same kind of data, but different constitutions, different preference data, different RL environments and different reward designs. The result: models that ask more or fewer questions, take more or less initiative, talk more or less tersely, abstain or guess under uncertainty. These differences are not accidents; they are the outcome of training decisions. In level 7 we will compare Claude and Codex through this lens.

Level 1: key takeaways

  • Pre-training gives knowledge, post-training gives behavior and character; RLVR is the engine of coding-agent ability.
  • A base model completes documents; the assistant is a character brought to life through a template.
  • Thinking tokens are billed as output even when invisible; they are steered with effort.
  • RL optimizes what is measured: reward hacking, sycophancy and confident guessing share the same root.
  • The model cannot reliably report what it knows or what it did; verification always comes from outside.
